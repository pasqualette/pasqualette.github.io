02 · The Business Problem
Paros wasn't profitable.

The root cause was organizational, not a market or strategy problem. Four symptoms made it visible.

SYMPTOM 01
Uncontrolled operational costs
Nobody could tell me how much toilet paper each school used in a month. Cost discipline had never been rewarded.
Root - Culture / Incentives
SYMPTOM 02
Paralyzed decision-making
Routine decisions took weeks - passing through layers of approval and informal stakeholder alignment.
Root - Architecture
SYMPTOM 03
No accountability for output
IT was the most visible example - delivery ran at roughly a third of capacity, with no mechanism holding anyone to what they'd committed.
Root - Networks / Routines
SYMPTOM 04
Financially unviable schools
Units in regions where the local labor market couldn't sustain them - the holding had no operational visibility into school-level realities.
Root - Architecture
03.1 · Architecture

Built for abundance, operating in scarcity.

A steep functional hierarchy - director, manager, coordinator, analyst - with centralized authority and low incentives. A design suited for large, capitalized companies, not a $10M business where the strategy had shifted to profitability.

HIERARCHY MAP · HOLDING → SCHOOLS
SilosDepartments optimized locally. No mechanism forced cross-functional coordination.
Informal veto powerAny legacy employee with institutional knowledge could block progress by withholding cooperation.
Stripped meaning at the edgeSchool staff felt it most - running classrooms with little say in the decisions shaping them.

03.2 · Culture · The Payment System Episode

When self-preservation overrode the mission.

A clear case of the underlying assumption operating at the very top - and how the political ground beneath the project shifted without me knowing.

ACT 01
The CEO gives me explicit carte blanche to replace the legacy payment system.
ACT 02
I line up buy-in from my boss and the heads of product, IT, and operations. Prepare a full proposal, distribute pre-reading, present to the C-suite.
ACT 03
The CEO rejects the proposal in front of the leadership team as if we'd never discussed it, and makes an example of me.
ACT 04
The real reason surfaces later:The current provider had close financial ties to the investing fund - and the CEO had only learned this AFTER giving me carte blanche.
ACT 05
He calls me privately to apologize.
Looking back, I had misread the field.
Stakeholders: legacy employees saw an opening to reshape the decision - they had more centrality than I did. My position: new to the company, no bridging ties to the quiet resisters who shaped the room.
The public reversal was a lesson in how much real decision-making lives in a network you're not part of - and a culture where it was always better to play safe.
03.3 · Networks · Information as Currency

How the self-preservation culture actually operated.

Architecture created the silos Functional walls separated departments by design.
Culture motivated the hoarding Self-preservation made information into a survival asset.
The network was where it played out The IT request flow is the clearest example. All technology demands across the company funneled through one product owner. She prioritized based on her perception of necessity - no transparent criteria, no priority matrix, no visibility for the requesters.
You had to convince her Departments needed to lobby the broker to advance their requests - the opposite of how a healthy product organization should operate.
DEPARTMENTS → PRODUCT OWNER (BROKER) → IT TEAM
03.4 · Routines

The routines that mattered most didn't exist.

Without formal routines, gatekeepers filled the vacuum. Broker power was a direct function of routine absence - the more critical the process that lacked structure, the more authority accrued to those who'd memorized how things actually got done.

Missing routine 01
Inventory tracking across schools
Operations had no monthly visibility into what each school was consuming. There was no single place where the data lived.
No aggregate data, no bulk-purchasing leverage. Savings lost on every consumable bought school by school.
Missing routine 02
Formal IT request process
No standardized intake. No transparent prioritization. Every demand had to be brokered through a single person.
Visibility, priority and timing all opaque to the rest of the organization - the perfect conditions for brokerage.
Missing routine 03
Cross-functional planning cadence
No scheduled rhythm for surfacing dependencies between departments. Coordination happened by goodwill or not at all.
Interdependencies surfaced only when they broke - and the fixes happened informally, reinforcing brokerage.

And brokerage was everywhere in Paros.

04 · Recommendations (1–3)

Five interconnected interventions across the OrgDNA.

01
Architecture · Information
Build the information infrastructure the organization is missing.
Without shared facts, every decision becomes a political negotiation. Unit-level dashboards tied to a monthly business-review routine.
  • Cost per student, inventory needs, basic operational data
  • Principals walk through their numbers monthly with leadership
  • Also tackles brokerage
02
Architecture · Structure
Make schools the center of gravity.
Shift the configuration toward a divisional logic - moving formal authority toward where the revenue is generated.
  • Principals as unit GMs with P&L responsibility
  • Holding functions reorganized around regional school clusters
  • Eliminate the redundant coordinator layer
03
Networks · Bridging
Engineer bridging ties holding ↔ schools.
The gap between the people designing tools and the people using them is a network problem - close the structural hole.
  • "School for a day" rotation, every quarter
  • Principals' council with high-influence ties to leadership
04 · Recommendations (4–5)

Routines and culture.

04
Routines + Status
Dismantle brokerage by formalizing the processes that enabled it.
Brokers gain power in the absence of formal routines. Closing those gaps is both a routine intervention and a status intervention - it removes the foundation gatekeepers stood on.
  • Transparent IT and product request routine with explicit prioritization criteria
  • Quarterly cross-functional planning cadence so interdependencies surface early
  • Phased ERP migration starting from the highest-friction integration points
  • Document system knowledge as it migrates - break the exclusive-knowledge status hierarchy
05
Culture + Influence
Realign status and incentives with the espoused mission.
The self-preservation assumption won't die unless leadership actively kills it - through several reinforcing mechanisms at once.
  • Visible cost discipline at the top: travel cut to a minimum, efficient teams publicly recognized
  • Real listening routines: pulse surveys, school visits, open forums
  • Incentives that reward what the old culture punished - paired with counter-metrics
  • Promote both archetypes: outsiders like Paul, insiders like Maria
  • Rewrite hiring, onboarding and promotion criteria to reproduce the new culture
05 · Bullet-proofing

Four risks. Four mitigations built into the plan.

Most change efforts fail not on strategy but on OB grounds - hearts, minds, networks, routines. So these are the risks worth flagging.

01
Reigniting the fear cycleChange on top of recent layoffs could reactivate self-protection.
Mitigation
Visible early wins - bulk-purchasing savings as concrete proof change is working.
02
Change overloadFive recommendations at once will exhaust the organization.
Mitigation
Phase the rollout in waves - don't introduce the next until the previous has settled.
03
Distorted incentivesCost discipline can become quality-damaging cost-cutting.
Mitigation
Pair every metric with a counter-metric. Cost/student ↔ student outcomes. Review at 6 mo.
04
Inconsistent signals from the topLeadership behavior is the one variable the effort can't fully control.
Mitigation
Distribute the "cultural carrier" role across multiple visible figures - change survives leadership inconsistency.
